## About page

> ### About Board Tech Benchmark
>
> Every board now has two jobs in technology: protecting the company, and making sure it uses technology well. The filings record the first in full and the second hardly at all.
>
> The Board Technology & Innovation Governance Benchmark reads what every company on Fortune's 2026 list has committed its board to in writing about technology: where oversight sits, what it is written to do, and how it is changing. We read proxy statements, annual reports and committee charters from 2011 to 2026, and place each board on a four-level scale. Every score traces to a sentence in a filing.
>
> Edition 1 found that 81.0% of Fortune 1000 boards govern technology as a risk, while 13.9% have a written mandate to create value with it. Fewer than 20 of the 1,000 can approve a technology plan or budget.
>
> **What you'll get**
>
> - **The quarterly update.** A short note on what changed: committees formed, renamed or dissolved, and charters rewritten.
> - **One finding at a time.** Shorter pieces on a single result, such as AI, the audit committee, or what a mandate actually authorises.
> - **Method notes.** What we measure, what we can't, and which way each limit pushes the numbers.
>
> **Who it's for**
>
> Directors, company secretaries and general counsel, governance and investor relations teams, investors, and anyone who advises boards on technology.
>
> **What we don't do**
>
> We don't name a company next to its score. A read of one board, verified by hand with the evidence behind it, goes only to that company, on request. We measure disclosed commitments, not boardroom behaviour, so every figure is a floor.

## First post outline

**Title:** Most boards still govern technology as a risk. Who owns the upside?

**Subtitle:** What the filings of the Fortune 1000 say about the board's second job in technology.

**Length:** about 1,500 words and 3 exhibits (release images 01, 02 and 04).

1. **Two jobs, one board (150 words).** Protecting the company, and making sure it uses technology well. The first has a complete form (a committee, a reporting executive and, since 2023, a regulatory deadline). The second is mostly blank.
2. **The headline (250 words, image 01).** 81.0% (810 of the 1,000) at levels 1 and 2; 13.9% (139) at levels 3 and 4; 51 not placed. Explain the four-level scale and the dividing line in plain words.
3. **Absorbed, not assigned (250 words, image 02).** 64.5% put technology under an audit or risk committee, 551 of them the audit committee itself. What that committee took on without changing its name.
4. **Written in, without teeth (250 words).** Cyber security is a duty in 99.7% of 957 scored mandates; strategy in 20.7%. 77.7% record no consequence. Fewer than 20 can approve a plan or budget. Then the limit: this does not show that boards lack influence.
5. **Where boards built something new (250 words, image 04).** 69.9% reach strategy with a dedicated committee; 2.9% without. Committees more than tripled since 2011. Then: no performance effect has been established (0 of 319 tests), and that is not evidence that committees have no value.
6. **AI (150 words).** 89.2% of annual reports name AI as a risk; 47.9% in the business section; 2 of 890 boards have their own AI committee.
7. **Four questions for your board (150 words).** Who owns technology's upside, and is it written down? How much of the audit committee's agenda does technology get? Does the mandate say what happens when a duty is not met? Who briefs the board on what to build?
8. **Method and what's next (100 words).** Filings read with LLM assistance; 165 boards re-read by hand, 93.9% agreement. Quarterly update; Russell 3000 work is preliminary and comes later.

**Before publishing:** check every figure against [fact-sheet.md](fact-sheet.md) and the final edition; no em dashes; British spelling; no company named.
